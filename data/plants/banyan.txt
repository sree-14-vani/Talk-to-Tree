Common Name: Banyan
Scientific Name: Ficus benghalensis
Plant Type: Tree
Family: Moraceae
Native Distribution: Indian subcontinent, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Pakistan
Habitat: Tropical and subtropical regions; grows in plains, low hills, near water bodies; often planted near temples and villages
Appearance: Large evergreen tree with spreading canopy; distinctive prop roots that grow from branches down to ground forming additional trunks; leathery oval leaves 10-20 cm long; small fig-like fruits (syconia) in pairs; bark grayish-brown and smooth
Growth Characteristics: Fast-growing; can reach 20-30 meters height; canopy spread often exceeds height; prop roots allow lateral expansion over vast areas; one of the largest trees by canopy coverage; long-lived, can survive several hundred years
Environmental Requirements: Full sun to partial shade; well-drained soil; tolerates variety of soil types including clay, loam, sand; drought tolerant once established; prefers warm humid climate; USDA zones 10-12
Ecological Importance: Keystone species in many ecosystems; fig fruits provide year-round food for birds, bats, primates, and other wildlife; dense canopy provides nesting sites and shelter; prop root systems stabilize soil and prevent erosion; creates microhabitats for epiphytes, insects, and small animals; significant carbon sequestration due to massive biomass
Role in Biodiversity: Supports over 100 bird species; fruits eaten by hornbills, pigeons, parrots, mynas, bats; fig wasp mutualism essential for pollination; canopy hosts orchids, ferns, mosses; root networks shelter reptiles and amphibians
Cultural Historical Significance: National tree of India; sacred in Hinduism and Buddhism; associated with Lord Shiva and Krishna; mentioned in ancient texts including Bhagavad Gita; village councils traditionally held under banyan trees; symbol of longevity, wisdom, and shelter; "banyan" derives from "baniya" (traders) who conducted business under its shade
Interesting Facts: The Great Banyan in Kolkata Botanical Garden covers 18,918 sq meters with over 3,700 prop roots; a single tree can resemble a forest; figs are inverted flowers (syconia) pollinated by specific wasp species; young leaves and buds edible; latex used in traditional medicine
Conservation Status: Not threatened; widely cultivated; some ancient specimens protected as heritage trees
General Care: Requires ample space for root spread; regular watering when young; minimal pruning needed; avoid planting near buildings due to aggressive roots; can be grown from seeds or cuttings
Safety Notes: Latex may cause skin irritation in sensitive individuals; do not consume any part without expert guidance; fruits generally safe when ripe but consult expert before medicinal use
